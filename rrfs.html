The model in detail

RRFS: a 3 km rapid refresh with an 84-hour horizon

NOAA’s Rapid Refresh Forecast System is the successor to the HRRR and the NAM. It runs on a 3 km grid over the continental United States, gets a new run every hour, keeps the 15-minute steps for the first 18 hours — and extends the forecast to 84 hours. A five-member ensemble, REFS, runs alongside it.

Where this model stands

RRFS is operational at NOAA and is replacing the RAP, the HRRR and the NAM in the production suite. RapidWind’s US layer still runs on the HRRR: the wind tiles and the point forecast come from the existing HRRR pipeline. This page describes the model that takes over; the map switches when the RRFS pipeline is live. Until then, every US link on this page opens the map with HRRR selected.

Why write about it before it is on the map? Because the change is substantial: a longer range, a different core, an ensemble in the same system — and the same 3 km, 15-minute detail that makes the US layer useful for session planning.

One system, several grids

RRFS is not a single run but a family that shares one code base, one physics package and one analysis chain:

  • CONUS, 3 km. A 1799 × 1059 Lambert conformal grid — the same projection and dimensions the HRRR used. Hourly cycles, 15-minute steps for 18 hours, hourly to 84 hours.
  • North America, 13 km. A 4881 × 2961 rotated latitude–longitude grid that covers Canada and Mexico, runs hourly to 84 hours and supplies the boundaries for the finer domains.
  • Alaska, 3 km. A polar stereographic grid (1649 × 1105) with the same hourly cadence and sub-hourly output as CONUS — unlike the old HRRR Alaska sector, which ran on a three-hourly cycle.
  • Hawaii and Puerto Rico, 2.5 km. Separate Mercator grids; no HRRR product existed for either before.
  • REFS, the ensemble. Five 3 km members to 60 hours at 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC. The same members contribute the flow-dependent part of the deterministic hybrid analysis.
  • Fire weather. A small, movable grid for fire behaviour, six-hourly.

For a wind-sport forecast the CONUS grid is the one that matters; the other domains explain why RRFS is a bigger change than “HRRR with more hours”.

What changed from the HRRR

  • Range: 18/48 hours became 84 hours. Every hourly run now carries three and a half days, not just the rest of today. The 15-minute detail still stops at hour 18.
  • One model instead of three. RRFS absorbs the RAP, the HRRR and the NAM nests. The HRRR and NAM feeds are retired at the end of November 2026.
  • A different core. RRFS is built on the FV3 limited-area core, the same dynamical-core family as the GFS, with physics derived from the HRRR/RAP lineage rather than a copy of the HRRR code.
  • An ensemble in the same system. REFS replaces the separate HREF/SREF collections with five 3 km members on the same grid, cycle and physics.
  • The same 3 km, 15-minute nowcast window. The first 18 hours are still published in 15-minute steps, which is where sea breeze, outflow and thermal breeze decide a session.

Why 3 km and 15 minutes still make a difference

Sea breeze and the coastline

A sea breeze is a circulation, not a gradient: it builds when the land heats faster than the water and collapses when the contrast fades. Its leading edge — the sea-breeze front — is a few kilometers wide. On a 13 km grid it is averaged away; on 3 km it survives, which is why coastal spots see a believable onset time instead of a smooth afternoon bump.

Terrain and channeled wind

The Columbia River Gorge is the classic case: a pressure difference across the Cascades pushes air through a narrow gap, and the shape of the walls decides where it accelerates. A convection-allowing grid resolves that channel; a coarse one smears it over the whole region. The same applies to canyons, headlands and the thermal breezes of the Great Lakes.

Convection, outflow and cold pools

Thunderstorms are not resolved by global models — they are parameterized, i.e. approximated from the surrounding conditions. At 3 km RRFS partly resolves updrafts, downdrafts and the cold pool that spreads out underneath a storm. That gust front is often the strongest wind of the day, and it is also the hardest to place in time.

Timing rather than strength

The most expensive error in session planning is rarely the wind speed; it is the clock. A front passes, the wind shifts inside half an hour, a storm cuts the thermal. An hourly reinitialization corrects those shifts far faster than a run that is six hours old — and the 84-hour range means the same model can also answer “what about the day after tomorrow?”, at hourly resolution.

Where RRFS stops

The limits are worth knowing:

  • 84 hours is the end, not a wall of accuracy. The last two days are a trend, not a session forecast. Detail also drops from 15 minutes to one hour after hour 18.
  • CONUS is a hard boundary in the CONUS file. Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico are separate grids; nothing outside the active grid is filled in.
  • No waves, no tide, no currents. The model carries wind, gusts, direction and weather — not sea state. For surf, swell or water level you need another source.
  • A 3 km grid does not see the beach. Dunes, seawalls, piers and trees are not in the grid. At the water’s edge the wind is gustier and more disturbed than 500 m offshore.
  • Cold pools and storm initiation remain hard. Where and when a cell first forms is still the weakest link, even at 3 km.
  • Five ensemble members are few. REFS shows spread and regime changes, but its probabilities are coarse. It is not a replacement for a 20-member ensemble.
  • The system is still settling. Product inventory, grid names and the parallel data tree were still marked preliminary in late 2026. Documentation can lag the running code.
And the most important limit: A forecast is still a forecast. Check the water, the sky and the local indicators — not just the number on screen.

How to read the map

The reading rules do not change when the US layer moves from HRRR to RRFS:

  • Color is mean wind speed, calibrated to the range that matters for kite, wing and windsurf — not to a general weather service scale. Values are in knots: 1 kn ≈ 1.15 mph (1.852 km/h), so 15 kn ≈ 17 mph.
  • Arrows show wind direction. Read them together with the degrees in the point forecast, which follow the meteorological convention: 270° means a west wind, blowing from the west.
  • Click the map for a point forecast — the time series at that coordinate, with gusts and a weather code.
  • Every timeline step is a real model step, not an interpolated in-between value: 15 minutes for the first 18 hours, one hour after that.
  • No station overlay in the US. The measurement layer is built from European stations, so the toggle stays disabled while a US model is active. For ground truth, use NDBC buoys or a nearby METAR station.
  • Gusts are maxima over the step interval — 15 minutes early on, one hour later. A calm hour can still contain a few spikes. Look at mean wind and gust.
  • Model switch, top left: ICON-D2 RUC for Germany and Central Europe, AROME PI for France, the US model for North America.

What the map does not show: no swell, no tide, no currents, no water temperature. The feed carries mean wind, gusts, direction, a weather code and day/night only.

How far to trust a run

An hourly run does not make a forecast certain. For session planning this gradation helps:

Reliability by lead time
Lead time Reliability What it allows
0–3 hours very high The analysis is fresh: you are watching reality more than a forecast.
3–6 hours high Sea-breeze onset and storm evolution are usually well timed.
6–18 hours high to moderate The 15-minute detail is still there; strength and timing can move.
18–48 hours moderate Pattern is right, details are hourly: plan the day, not the gust.
48–84 hours low to moderate A trend for the trip; re-check on the morning of the session.

Past the first few hours the sub-hourly detail is gone: the run is still hourly and still 3 km, but it is no longer a nowcast of the next gust front.

Not in the US? Which model to use

RapidWind carries three models with different, only partly overlapping areas. Where no model reaches, the map stays blank — that means “no model”, not “no wind”.

RRFS model FAQ

How often does RRFS update?

Every hour, all day. Each deterministic run goes out 84 hours: 15-minute steps for the first 18 hours, hourly after that. The five-member REFS ensemble runs four times a day (00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC) to 60 hours. Bottom left, the line “Updated … | Next ~…” gives the last update and the estimated next run.

What output interval does RRFS use?

15 minutes for the first 18 hours, hourly from hour 19 to hour 84. For wind sports the 15-minute part is the interesting one: it is where sea breeze, outflow and thermal breeze change fastest. The longer hourly part is for planning the next days, not for timing a gust front.

Does RRFS replace the HRRR and the NAM?

Yes. RRFS is the operational successor to the RAP, the HRRR and the NAM nests. The HRRR and NAM feeds are being retired at the end of November 2026; Open-Meteo already carries RRFS as their replacement. RapidWind's US layer still uses HRRR and will move to RRFS when the new pipeline is live.

Does RRFS cover Alaska, Hawaii and Puerto Rico?

Yes, as separate grids: Alaska at 3 km, Hawaii and Puerto Rico at 2.5 km. They are not part of the CONUS file, so a map of the continental United States shows no data outside its own grid. A 13 km North America grid covers Canada and Mexico and supplies the boundaries.

Is RRFS a global model?

No. It is a limited-area system built on the FV3 dynamical core. The 13 km North America grid provides the outer boundary, and the global GFS supplies the environment beyond it. Outside the RRFS domains there is no data, which is why RapidWind uses separate models for Europe.

Is there an RRFS ensemble?

Yes: REFS, the Rapid Ensemble Forecast System, with five 3 km members to 60 hours at 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC. The members also feed the hybrid analysis of the deterministic run. Five members is a small ensemble — enough to show spread, not enough for sharp probabilities.

Why does my forecast differ from another app?

Usually because of the model, the grid or the grid cell: the value comes from the nearest 3 km cell, not from a measurement at the beach. In sea-breeze regimes and around headlands two neighboring cells can differ noticeably. Gusts are also less certain than the mean wind.

Is the data free to use?

Yes. RRFS output is NOAA open data and free to use, with attribution to NOAA/NCEP. RapidWind is free as well: no login, no ads.

What about Europe or the UK?

RRFS has no data there. RapidWind carries two European models instead: ICON-D2 RUC for Germany and Central Europe, and AROME PI for France and its neighbors. Spots in the UK and Ireland fall outside all three model areas.

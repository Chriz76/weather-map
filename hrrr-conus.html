The model in detail

HRRR: a 3 km wind forecast, recalculated every hour

NOAA’s High-Resolution Rapid Refresh is a convection-allowing model on a 3 km grid over the contiguous United States. It gets a new run every hour, starts from an analysis that includes the last hour of radar data, and puts out 15-minute steps for the first 18 hours. That is exactly the window where sea breeze, outflow and thermal breeze decide whether a session happens.

Rapid refresh: two run lengths, one model

The HRRR produces two kinds of run from the same system:

  • Hourly runs, 18 hours out. Every hour of the day, a new forecast that covers the next 18 hours in 15-minute steps. This is the part that matters for a session today.
  • Four long runs, 48 hours out. At 00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC the model extends to two days, hourly. Good for planning tomorrow, coarser in time.

The engine behind it is a convection-allowing setup: radar reflectivity is assimilated every 15 minutes over a one-hour window, on the same 3 km grid the forecast runs on, and the background comes from the hourly analysis of the coarser RAP model. Since version 4 the initialization also uses a 36-member, 3 km ensemble (HRRRDAS) with direct reflectivity assimilation — that is what lets the model start a thunderstorm where the radar already sees rain, instead of waiting for a global model to notice.

Why 3 km and 15 minutes make a difference

Sea breeze and the coastline

A sea breeze is a circulation, not a gradient: it builds when the land heats faster than the water and collapses when the contrast fades. Its leading edge — the sea-breeze front — is a few kilometers wide. On a 13 km grid it is averaged away; on 3 km it survives, which is why coastal spots see a believable onset time instead of a smooth afternoon bump.

Terrain and channeled wind

The Columbia River Gorge is the classic case: a pressure difference across the Cascades pushes air through a narrow gap, and the shape of the walls decides where it accelerates. A convection-allowing grid resolves that channel; a coarse one smears it over the whole region. The same applies to canyons, headlands and the thermal breezes of the Great Lakes.

Convection, outflow and cold pools

Thunderstorms are not resolved by global models — they are parameterized, i.e. approximated from the surrounding conditions. At 3 km the HRRR partly resolves updrafts, downdrafts and the cold pool that spreads out underneath a storm. That gust front is often the strongest wind of the day, and it is also the hardest to place in time.

Timing rather than strength

The most expensive error in session planning is rarely the wind speed; it is the clock. A front passes, the wind shifts inside half an hour, a storm cuts the thermal. A run reinitialized every hour corrects those shifts far faster than a run that is six hours old.

Where the HRRR stops

The limits are worth knowing:

  • CONUS is a hard boundary. Alaska runs as a separate HRRR sector on a three-hourly cycle; Hawaii and Puerto Rico have no HRRR product at all. Nothing outside the grid is filled in.
  • 18 hours at 15 minutes, then hourly. Beyond hour 18 the output interval coarsens and the detail you came for is gone.
  • No waves, no tide, no currents. The model carries wind, gusts, direction and weather — not sea state. For surf, swell or water level you need another source.
  • A 3 km grid does not see the beach. Dunes, seawalls, piers and trees are not in the grid. At the water’s edge the wind is gustier and more disturbed than 500 m offshore.
  • Cold pools and storm initiation remain hard. Where and when a cell first forms is still the weakest link, even at 3 km.
  • No operational ensemble. The HRRRDAS ensemble is used for data assimilation; there is no public HRRR forecast ensemble. A single run is a single scenario.
  • A successor is in the works. RRFS is designed to replace RAP, HRRR and NAM; until it is declared operational, HRRR remains the product to use.
And the most important limit: A forecast is still a forecast. Check the water, the sky and the local indicators — not just the number on screen.

Gusts and storms: what the model knows, what it does not

The gust value shown is the maximum over the step interval — 15 minutes in the first 18 hours, one hour later on. It gives you an idea of the peak, not a promise about what you will get at the water’s edge. Three situations come up again and again:

  • Established sea breeze — modest gusts, steady wind: the model is usually reliable, and the 15-minute steps show the onset.
  • Storm outflow — the gust front can double the wind in minutes and shift the direction sharply. The HRRR often shows the cold pool, rarely its exact arrival time.
  • Channeled wind (Gorge, canyons) — the acceleration is captured well; the local effect of a wing dam, pier or bluff is not in the grid.

How to read the map

  • Color is mean wind speed, calibrated to the range that matters for kite, wing and windsurf — not to a general weather service scale. Values are in knots: 1 kn ≈ 1.15 mph (1.852 km/h), so 15 kn ≈ 17 mph.
  • Arrows show wind direction. Read them together with the degrees in the point forecast, which follow the meteorological convention: 270° means a west wind, blowing from the west.
  • Click the map for a point forecast — the time series at that coordinate, with gusts and a weather code.
  • Every timeline step is a real model step, not an interpolated in-between value: 15 minutes for the first 18 hours, one hour after that.
  • No station overlay with HRRR. The measurement layer is built from European stations, so the toggle stays disabled while HRRR is active. For ground truth in the US, use NDBC buoys or a nearby METAR station.
  • Gusts are maxima over the step interval — 15 minutes early on, one hour later. A calm hour can still contain a few spikes. Look at mean wind and gust.
  • Model switch, top left: ICON-D2 RUC for Germany and Central Europe, AROME PI for France, HRRR for the US.

What the map does not show: no swell, no tide, no currents, no water temperature. The feed carries mean wind, gusts, direction, a weather code and day/night only.

How far to trust a run

An hourly run does not make a forecast certain. For session planning this gradation helps:

Reliability by lead time
Lead time Reliability What it allows
0–3 hours very high The analysis is fresh: you are watching reality more than a forecast.
3–6 hours high Sea-breeze onset and storm evolution are usually well timed.
6–18 hours moderate Pattern is right, strength and timing can still move.
18–48 hours low to moderate Read as a trend and re-check on the morning of the session.

Past the first few hours the 15-minute detail is gone: the run is still hourly and still 3 km, but it is no longer a nowcast of the next gust front.

Not in the US? Which model to use

RapidWind carries three models with different, only partly overlapping areas. Where no model reaches, the map stays blank — that means “no model”, not “no wind”.

HRRR model FAQ

How often does the HRRR update?

Every hour, all day. Each run starts from a fresh analysis that includes radar reflectivity from the past hour. The 48-hour versions run four times a day (00, 06, 12 and 18 UTC); every other hour produces an 18-hour forecast. Bottom left, the line “Updated … | Next ~…” gives the last update and the estimated next run; click it to see the exact model run and the last sync.

What output interval does the HRRR use?

15 minutes for the first 18 hours, hourly after that up to 48 hours. For wind sports the 15-minute part is the interesting one: it is where sea breeze, outflow and thermal breeze change fastest.

Does HRRR cover Hawaii, Alaska or Puerto Rico?

No. The CONUS HRRR covers the contiguous United States only. Alaska runs as a separate HRRR sector on a three-hourly cycle; Hawaii and Puerto Rico have no HRRR product at all. The RapidWind map therefore shows HRRR for CONUS spots only.

Is HRRR a global model?

No. It is a limited-area, convection-allowing model: a 3 km grid over CONUS, initialized from the RAP analysis, which in turn is driven by the GFS. Outside its domain there is no data, which is why RapidWind uses separate models for Europe.

Why does my forecast differ from another app?

Usually because of the model, the grid or the grid cell: the value comes from the nearest 3 km cell, not from a measurement at the beach. In sea-breeze regimes and around headlands two neighboring cells can differ noticeably. Gusts are also less certain than the mean wind.

Is the data free to use?

Yes. HRRR output is NOAA open data and free to use, with attribution to NOAA/NCEP. RapidWind is free as well: no login, no ads.

What about Europe or the UK?

HRRR has no data there. RapidWind carries two European models instead: ICON-D2 RUC for Germany and Central Europe, and AROME PI for France and its neighbors. Spots in the UK and Ireland fall outside all three model areas.
